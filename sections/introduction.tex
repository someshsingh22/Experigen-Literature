\section{Introduction}
Autoresearch systems automate the cycle of proposing ideas, running experiments, and using their outcomes to guide further investigation \citep{karpathy2026autoresearch}. Yet an agent can produce a new hypothesis at each iteration while repeatedly revisiting the same explanation. This lack of hypothesis diversity limits the value of an otherwise productive research loop: the bank grows, but the range of relationships considered may change little. The distinction is consequential in social science, where testing a proposal can require operationalizing a construct, accounting for confounders, or collecting additional observations. Repeatedly examining closely related accounts leaves less capacity to investigate alternatives. A useful discovery system should therefore offer researchers a range of distinct, testable explanations, alongside evidence about which of them predict the observed outcomes.

Recent methods provide increasingly capable tools for generating hypotheses from data. HypoGeniC iteratively refines natural-language hypotheses using labeled observations \citep{zhou2024hypothesis}, while ExperiGen connects generation to statistical testing and experimental feedback \citep{sengupta2026experigen}. These advances help assess and improve individual proposals. The quality of a hypothesis bank also depends on what its proposals collectively cover. For a system studying persuasion, hypotheses about citing sources and supplying external evidence may describe much the same relationship. Both could predict successful counterarguments, yet neither examines whether acknowledging another person's concerns or appealing to shared values also matters. Predictive performance alone cannot establish whether a system has explored these alternatives.

The \emph{Artificial Hivemind} study gives a broader reason to examine this issue: language models often produce similar responses both within repeated samples and across model families \citep{jiang2025artificialhivemind}. Our concern is the diversity of hypotheses generated from social-science data. In this setting, useful variation lies in the constructs and proposed mechanisms that a researcher could investigate. Differences in wording are insufficient when they leave the underlying account unchanged. We examine whether this convergence also appears in data-grounded hypothesis generation, and how persona conditioning relates to explanatory diversity and predictive utility.

Persona conditioning offers a way to vary the perspectives from which a model approaches the same material. Persona Hub uses descriptions of people's backgrounds and interests to steer diverse synthetic generation \citep{ge2024personahub}, and Social Agents aggregates independent persona judgments for behavioral prediction \citep{bhattacharyya2026social}. These findings motivate a related question for scientific discovery: can persona information change which explanations an agent proposes from a fixed dataset? This makes the content of a profile a substantive choice. A description relevant to the behavior under study may suggest relationships that a generic prompt overlooks; additional biographical detail may also introduce variation with little empirical value. We therefore treat persona population size, descriptive attributes, and dataset relevance as experimental choices whose effects on diversity and utility must be measured.

We draw profiles from CMV author personas \citep{yuCmvAuthorPersonas} and Twin-2K-500 \citep{toubia2025twin2k500} to condition hypothesis generation, using \emph{farthest-first} selection to retain proposals that add semantic novelty to a shared memory bank. We evaluate on ChangeMyView (CMV) persuasion, Am I the Asshole (AITA) community judgments, and news headline selection. Normalized embedding-based \emph{Vendi} \citep{friedman2023vendi} and LLM-judged \emph{Construct Diversity} assess hypothesis diversity; ROC-AUC and F1 assess predictive utility.

We find substantial convergence in the hypotheses generated under the conditions we study \somesh{This is curtailing your contributions}. Without personas, normalized \somesh{Never bring unintroudced terms}Vendi is approximately 21\%; with persona conditioning, it rises to approximately 31\%, compared with 66.67\% for human-generated hypotheses. Detailed profiles relevant to the dataset yield more diverse hypotheses than sparse or less relevant profiles, and attribute-removal ablations suggest that profile content matters. Yet providing different profiles does not guarantee diverse collective output: a persona council, in which agents collaborate on their proposals, remains near 20\%. Together, these observations show that measured diversity varies with the information and interaction setting used for generation, while convergence persists across the conditions examined.

Dataset-relevant detail also improves ROC-AUC in the comparisons we evaluate, but the configurations with the greatest diversity do not consistently achieve the highest ROC-AUC or F1. Choosing a persona population therefore involves balancing the range of explanations it produces with their predictive usefulness. These findings highlight the importance of examining both the diversity and predictive utility of hypothesis banks when studying convergence in agentic research.

\section{Methodology}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We start with selecting the baselines, first would be zero shot llm (no persona), second would be cmv aligned demographics,third cluster summary from the cmv dataset and two twin 2k baselines where in one we use demographics only and 2nd is more detailed with salary, religious belief, family size, etc.
    \item Now as an input we pass a dataset, the personas and we initialize our memory bank (t) which at this stage is empty
    \item Next we pass these 3 components to the Generator LLM as inputs which is supposed to use these to generate hypotheses,using roleplay prompting
    \item For each baseline we have 30 personas sampled which are representative of the dataset now we run different conditions where no. of personas (n) is tested at 5,10,15,20,25,30.
    \item Each persona generates exactly one hypotheses. So for example n=5 will generate 5 hypotheses and we'll pick one from those 5 which will be semantically farthest i.e maximum novel and increment the memory bank which now becomes 1. This operation is run iteratively till our bank becomes of size 30.  
    \item The role of hypotheses appending in the memory is to get passed as user template for the next round where we ask the llm to not generate hypotheses that has this mechanism
    \item Finally we record the diversity at t=10,20,30 bank size and measure the diversity using a metric called as Vendi Score.
    \item Vendi score = effective number of distinct hypotheses in a bank (exp of the entropy of the embedding similarity matrix's eigenvalues). A bank of 30 near-duplicates scores ~1; 30 genuinely different ideas score ~30.

\subsection{Persona Scaling — Open RQs, TODOs \& Ideas}

\paragraph{Research Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Persona Count):} How does the number of personas affect the diversity \emph{and} accuracy of generated hypotheses? What is the optimal number of personas?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Persona Detail):} How detailed should personas be for this task? Which attributes are most important? What is the best $(\text{size} \times \text{attributes})$ configuration?
    \emph{(RQ2 is harder and downstream of RQ1.)}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Current Finding}
Personas improve hypothesis diversity; more target-population-informed personas yield better diversity. However, diversity $\neq$ accuracy — random personas would be diverse but inaccurate. Dataset knowledge is essential for accuracy.
