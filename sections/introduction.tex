\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Researchers in data-driven sciences use observations to formulate and test explanations for human behavior. LLM-based research agents increasingly automate this process, combining pretrained knowledge with supplied datasets to generate hypotheses and refine them through experimental feedback \citep{zhou2024hypothesis,sengupta2026experigen}. However, language models exhibit an \emph{Artificial Hivemind}, producing similar ideas both within and across models \citep{jiang2025artificialhivemind}. Scientific datasets introduce evidence specific to the phenomenon being studied, potentially revealing relationships beyond the model's familiar explanations. Whether this convergence persists when generation is grounded in empirical observations remains unclear. In this work, we establish that an \emph{agentic hivemind} persists in data-driven sciences: agents repeatedly propose overlapping ideas and hypotheses, and increasing the amount of input data does not meaningfully broaden their diversity. When testing these proposals requires costly analysis, data collection, or experimentation, convergence consumes limited research resources while leaving alternative explanations unexplored.

More observations, repeated experimentation, and multiple agents offer natural ways to broaden scientific exploration. Additional data may reveal overlooked relationships, feedback may redirect the search, and different generators may contribute complementary ideas. Their value depends on whether they bring distinct explanations into consideration. For a system studying persuasion, hypotheses about citing sources, supplying evidence, and supporting claims with references may all describe essentially the same relationship. A proposal about acknowledging another person's concerns would introduce a different account. A collection of individually useful hypotheses can therefore remain narrow in what it explains. The Artificial Hivemind study documents convergence in responses to open-ended queries \citep{jiang2025artificialhivemind}; we examine its manifestation in scientific inquiry, where hypotheses are grounded in observations and their evaluation consumes a finite research budget.

We systematically study hypothesis diversity across tasks, models, and generation procedures. Our experiments reveal \emph{intra-model repetition}: repeated generations revisit similar constructs and explanatory relationships, even as the amount of input data increases. They also reveal \emph{inter-model homogeneity}: different models independently produce overlapping hypotheses, limiting the additional diversity obtained by changing generators. We examine iterative search and multi-agent generation within this evaluation, asking how effectively they expand the range of explanations considered. The iterative methods we evaluate remain limited in their ability to escape this concentration, exposing a mismatch between continued hypothesis generation and broader exploration when verification is expensive.

We evaluate hypothesis generation on persuasion in ChangeMyView discussions, community judgments of interpersonal situations in Am I the Asshole, and selection between paired news headlines. Our evaluation examines whether generated hypotheses introduce distinct explanatory relationships and whether they predict observed outcomes. We compare these explanations with human-generated hypotheses to assess the breadth of scientific ideas explored by research agents.

Together, these findings characterize an agentic hivemind in data-driven scientific discovery and show its persistence as we increase data, change models, and iterate over proposals. They also identify a practical limitation of evaluating research agents through hypothesis counts or predictive performance alone: both can obscure how narrowly a system searches for explanations. For researchers deploying these systems, the relevant question is how much useful explanatory ground each additional generation and verification step covers. Evaluating that coverage alongside empirical validity is necessary to understand the scientific value of increasingly automated research.

\section{Methodology}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We start with selecting the baselines, first would be zero shot llm (no persona), second would be cmv aligned demographics,third cluster summary from the cmv dataset and two twin 2k baselines where in one we use demographics only and 2nd is more detailed with salary, religious belief, family size, etc.
    \item Now as an input we pass a dataset, the personas and we initialize our memory bank (t) which at this stage is empty
    \item Next we pass these 3 components to the Generator LLM as inputs which is supposed to use these to generate hypotheses,using roleplay prompting
    \item For each baseline we have 30 personas sampled which are representative of the dataset now we run different conditions where no. of personas (n) is tested at 5,10,15,20,25,30.
    \item Each persona generates exactly one hypotheses. So for example n=5 will generate 5 hypotheses and we'll pick one from those 5 which will be semantically farthest i.e maximum novel and increment the memory bank which now becomes 1. This operation is run iteratively till our bank becomes of size 30.  
    \item The role of hypotheses appending in the memory is to get passed as user template for the next round where we ask the llm to not generate hypotheses that has this mechanism
    \item Finally we record the diversity at t=10,20,30 bank size and measure the diversity using a metric called as Vendi Score.
    \item Vendi score = effective number of distinct hypotheses in a bank (exp of the entropy of the embedding similarity matrix's eigenvalues). A bank of 30 near-duplicates scores ~1; 30 genuinely different ideas score ~30.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Persona Scaling — Open RQs, TODOs \& Ideas}

\paragraph{Research Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Persona Count):} How does the number of personas affect the diversity \emph{and} accuracy of generated hypotheses? What is the optimal number of personas?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Persona Detail):} How detailed should personas be for this task? Which attributes are most important? What is the best $(\text{size} \times \text{attributes})$ configuration?
    \emph{(RQ2 is harder and downstream of RQ1.)}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Current Finding}
Personas improve hypothesis diversity; more target-population-informed personas yield better diversity. However, diversity $\neq$ accuracy — random personas would be diverse but inaccurate. Dataset knowledge is essential for accuracy.
